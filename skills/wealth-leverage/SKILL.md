# Wealth & Leverage Frameworks — Naval, Horowitz

Sources: Naval Ravikant (Naval Almanack + podcast corpus), Ben Horowitz (The Hard Thing About Hard Things + What You Do Is Who You Are)

---

## Naval Ravikant — Wealth Creation

### The Wealth Equation

> "Wealth is assets that earn while you sleep."

Wages are not wealth. A salary produces money in exchange for time. Wealth produces money in exchange for nothing — it runs while you do something else. The goal is to build systems, products, businesses, and positions that generate value without your continuous labor.

**The components:**

`Wealth = Specific Knowledge × Accountability × Leverage`

Each multiplier compounds the others. Zero in any one component means zero output from the equation, regardless of the others.

---

### Specific Knowledge

> "Specific knowledge is knowledge you cannot be trained for. If society can train you for it, society can train someone else and replace you."

Specific knowledge is acquired through:
- Following your genuine curiosity (not a curriculum)
- Building skills in areas where you have unusual advantages or unusual interest
- Combining skills in ways that are rare (not just being good at one common thing)

**How to identify your specific knowledge:**
- What do you find easy that others find hard?
- What do you find interesting that others find boring?
- What combination of skills do you have that almost nobody else has simultaneously?

**The test:** Can your specific knowledge be turned into a job listing with a salary? If yes, it's not specific enough — it's a commodity skill. Specific knowledge cannot be hired because it can't be fully specified.

**Examples:**
- Being able to code AND design AND sell (all three together = rare; any one alone = commodity)
- Deep domain knowledge in a niche industry + the ability to build software for it
- Knowing both the technical architecture of a system AND the operational process it serves

**Building specific knowledge:**
- Read obsessively in 2–3 adjacent domains
- Build things at the intersection of those domains
- Teach what you know publicly — teaching reveals gaps and builds reputation simultaneously

---

### Accountability

> "Arm yourself with specific knowledge, accountability, and leverage. If you aren't being accountable, you're not taking enough risk."

Accountability means operating under your own name and taking personal responsibility for outcomes — both success and failure.

**Why accountability creates wealth:**
- Without accountability, there is no upside asymmetry. Salaried employees share company risk but not company equity.
- Accountability is what allows equity, profit sharing, and reputation accumulation
- Your name is your brand. The more visible the name, the greater the leverage of every output.

**Accountability spectrum:**
- No accountability: anonymous work, employed under someone else's name
- Mid-level: employee whose output is attributed to them within the company
- High accountability: founder, author, creator whose name is attached to everything they produce

**Risk tolerance required:** High accountability means your failures are visible. This deters most people — which is precisely why high accountability is a competitive advantage.

### The 4 Types of Leverage

Leverage is the mechanism that decouples your output from your time.

| Type | Description | Permissionless? | Key Feature |
|------|-------------|----------------|-------------|
| **Labor** | Other people working for you | No — requires convincing others | Oldest form. Least scalable. Management is its own full-time job. |
| **Capital** | Money working for you | No — requires someone to give it | Powerful but requires proof of track record first |
| **Code** | Software running without you | Yes | Zero marginal cost to serve additional users. The most powerful leverage available today for those who can build. |
| **Media** | Content operating without you | Yes | Podcast, book, newsletter, video. Asymmetric: one piece of content can influence millions at zero additional cost. |

**The permissionless distinction is critical.** Labor requires people to agree to work for you. Capital requires investors to agree to give it to you. Code and Media can be deployed by anyone, without permission, without institutional backing.

**Naval's insight:** "Fortunes require leverage. Business leverage comes from capital, people, and products with no marginal cost of replication. The most interesting and underused leverage is ideas in code and media."

**For founders without capital:** Build leverage through Code and Media first. These compound. Capital follows demonstrated leverage.

### Naval's 3 Decision Heuristics

**Heuristic 1 — Long-term games:**
> "Play long-term games with long-term people."

Wealth compounds through repeated, trusted interactions. The person you do business with repeatedly is more valuable than the person you do business with once. Every transaction with a long-term player is an investment. Every transaction with a short-term player is a risk.

**Application:** Optimise your professional environment for playing repeated games with the same people. One honest long-term relationship outperforms ten one-shot transactions.

**Heuristic 2 — Find the right game:**
> "You're not going to get rich renting out your time. You must own equity — a piece of a business — to gain your financial freedom."

The structure of your participation in value creation determines the ceiling on your wealth. Find structures where your upside is unbounded.

**Application:** Salary with upside potential < equity in something that compounds < permissionless leverage that you fully own.

**Heuristic 3 — Compounding identity:**
> "Become the best in the world at what you do. Keep redefining what you do until this is true."

You don't need to be the best accountant, or the best programmer, or the best salesperson. You need to be the best at the specific combination you occupy. Narrow the definition until you are genuinely at the top.

**Application:** Keep narrowing your positioning until you have no comparable competition. "NDIS AI agent installation with guaranteed outcomes" is narrower and more defensible than "AI automation for SMEs."

## Ben Horowitz — The Hard Thing About Hard Things

### Peacetime vs. Wartime CEO

The most important CEO framework for behavioral clarity. Most leadership advice is written for peacetime. Most startups spend significant periods in wartime. Applying peacetime principles in wartime kills companies.

**Definition:**
- **Peacetime:** The company has a significant advantage over competitors in its core market. Energy can be spent on expanding the lead, culture-building, and optimizing.
- **Wartime:** The company is fighting for survival against a serious threat — a competitor with superior resources, a market shift, a technology disruption, a cash crisis, a regulatory threat.

**Diagnostic question:** "Is the primary constraint on our progress internal optimisation, or external threat?" If external threat: wartime. If internal optimisation: peacetime.

---

**15-Dimension Comparison:**

| Dimension | Peacetime CEO | Wartime CEO |
|-----------|--------------|-------------|
| **Focus** | Broad organisation health | Single existential priority |
| **Decision speed** | Deliberate, consensus-oriented | Fast, unilateral when needed |
| **Rule adherence** | Builds and enforces processes | Breaks rules when survival requires it |
| **Communication style** | Inclusive, diplomatic | Terse, direct, brutal clarity |
| **Hierarchy** | Respects chain of command | Goes around it when necessary |
| **Failure tolerance** | Forgives mistakes generously | Intolerant of mission-critical failures |
| **Culture-building** | Active, prioritized | Deferred — mission first |
| **Team morale** | High-investment, protected | Secondary to mission outcomes |
| **Onboarding new people** | Extensive, gradual | Immediate full pressure |
| **Innovation** | Encouraged broadly | Focused narrowly on what's existential |
| **Market expansion** | Active, exploratory | Deprioritized — hold what you have |
| **Competition** | Monitors, outflanks | Studies obsessively, fights directly |
| **Transparency** | High, broad information sharing | Need-to-know basis for sensitive intel |
| **Time horizon** | Long-term compounding | The next 30–90 days |
| **Success metric** | Market share, NPS, employee happiness | Survival, cash flow, the win condition |

**The trap:** Founders who survive wartime sometimes cannot switch back to peacetime principles when the company stabilises. Wartime behaviors in peacetime produce burnout, attrition, and culture destruction.

### The Struggle

Horowitz's honest description of what building a company actually feels like, not what it looks like in retrospect.

> "The Struggle is not failure, but it causes failure. Mediocre founders let The Struggle stop them. Great founders allow The Struggle to change them."

**The Struggle is:**
- When the thing you built is not working
- When you don't know whether to pivot or persist
- When your team is looking at you for answers you don't have
- When the gap between what you said would happen and what is happening is undeniable

**What not to do in The Struggle:**
1. Don't put it all on the line for a Hail Mary you know is low-probability
2. Don't give up from exhaustion alone — The Struggle feels permanent, it isn't
3. Don't lie to yourself — the situation is what it is; denial compounds the problem
4. Don't go it alone — use your board, advisors, and investors for what they actually know

**What to do:**
- Get the problem out of your head and into the open — externalize it
- Find the one person who has genuinely solved this problem and talk to them
- Focus on what is working, not only on what is broken
- Look for the asymmetric upside in the constraint — crises create options that comfort does not

### Hiring Principles (Horowitz)

**The single most important thing a CEO does:** Horowitz is explicit — hiring is the job. Everything else is recoverable. The wrong people in the wrong roles is not.

**Principle 1 — Define the position before filling it**

Write the spec before meeting candidates. A spec includes:
- The 3–5 things this person will be accountable for in the first 90 days
- The skill set required (not desired — required)
- The specific failure mode they must not have (what would make this hire disastrous?)

**Principle 2 — Know the difference between the right person and your first choice**

The person you like most, who makes the best impression in the interview, is often not the right person. Structured reference calls and practical tasks reveal more than interviews.

**Principle 3 — Interview for the specific job, not for general excellence**

A world-class VP of Sales from a Fortune 500 company is not the right VP of Sales for a 10-person startup. The skills are different. The context is different. Excellence in one does not predict excellence in the other.

**Principle 4 — Take references seriously**

Most founders collect reference names but don't call them, or call them and accept vague positive answers. The right way to take a reference:
- Ask specifically about failures: "Can you describe a time they struggled?"
- Ask about management style: "How did they respond when they disagreed with a decision?"
- Ask about the actual job: "We're asking them to do X. What parts of X do you think will be hardest for them?"

**Principle 5 — Hire to the right role, not to fill the org chart**

The right person for where the company is now may not be the right person for where it will be in 2 years. Be honest about which hire you're making. Promising someone a future that belongs to someone else is destructive to everyone.

### The A/B Test on Culture

Horowitz's model for what company culture actually is versus what companies say it is:

> "Culture is not what you say. It is what you do. Specifically, it is who you promote and who you fire."

If you promote someone for results achieved through behavior that violates your stated values, your actual values are the behavior — not the stated values. The team reads actions, not words.

**Application:**
- Every promotion decision is a culture statement
- Every firing decision is a culture statement
- Every tolerance of bad behavior (regardless of performance) is a culture statement

What you do when it's expensive to do the right thing is what your culture actually is.
