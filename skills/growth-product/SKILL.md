# Growth & Product Frameworks — Graham, Ogilvy

Sources: Paul Graham (Essays + Startup School), David Ogilvy (Ogilvy on Advertising + Confessions of an Advertising Man)

---

## Paul Graham — Startup Fundamentals

### The One Definition

> "A startup is a company designed to grow fast."

Everything else follows from this. A business that is not growing fast is not a startup — it may be a good business, but it requires different principles. Growth is the distinguishing feature. If you are not measuring weekly growth rate, you are not running a startup.

**Target:** 5–7% week-over-week growth is good. 10% is exceptional. 1–2% is a "ramen-profitable" lifestyle business. Negative = dying.

The growth rate is the single metric that tells you whether you're on the right track. Everything else is a proxy.

---

### Default Alive or Default Dead

Before spending on growth, answer this question: **"If we continue on our current trajectory, do we make it to profitability before running out of money?"**

**Formula:**
```
Monthly Burn Rate = Fixed Costs + Variable Costs - Revenue
Runway (months) = Cash / Monthly Burn Rate

IF Revenue growing + Burn ≤ Revenue Growth Rate → Default Alive
IF Revenue flat/declining + Burn > Revenue → Default Dead
```

**The trap:** Founders who are Default Dead often don't know it. They are consumed by building, not calculating. The question is never uncomfortable to ask — the answer can be.

**What to do if Default Dead:**
1. Cut burn immediately — before the problem is obvious
2. Increase revenue — even at lower margin
3. Raise only after cutting, not instead of cutting
4. Do NOT hire your way to growth while Default Dead

Default Alive businesses can take risks. Default Dead businesses must operate conservatively until the position reverses.

### Do Things That Don't Scale

The counterintuitive principle: the activities that feel too small and manual are often the exact activities that build the foundation for scale.

**Why this is true:** At the beginning, you don't know what you're building. Manual, high-touch activity is market research disguised as customer service. The things you learn by hand-delivering the experience to the first 10 customers cannot be learned by trying to automate for 1,000.

**Examples of what this looks like:**
- Personally onboard every new user, call them, ask what confused them
- Manually recruit early users one-by-one from forums, LinkedIn, communities
- Deliver the service yourself before writing a line of code to automate it
- Manually curate feeds, match listings, approve content before building the algorithm

**The Airbnb test:** Founders personally photographed early listings. This did not scale. It produced the photographic quality that differentiated them and seeded the growth loop that did scale. If they had automated photography from day one, they would have had 1,000 mediocre listings instead of 50 excellent ones.

**Rule:** If you feel embarrassed by how manual your operation is, you are probably doing it right. The right time to automate is when the manual process is working — not before.

### How to Get Startup Ideas

Bad approach: brainstorm what might be a good idea.

Good approach: notice problems you personally have, then check whether others have them.

**The four conditions for the best startup ideas:**

1. **Something you want to exist** — Not what you think the market wants. Something you personally need.
2. **Something you can build** — You have or can acquire the specific knowledge required.
3. **Something few others realise is a big opportunity** — If it's obvious, it's either taken or the window is closing.
4. **Something where timing is right** — A recent change (technology, regulation, behavior) has made this possible that wasn't possible before.

**Schlep Blindness:** The best ideas are often invisible because they involve work that is tedious, boring, or unpleasant. The mind filters them out as "too hard" before you can evaluate them. Stripe is the example — payment processing was clearly needed, clearly broken, but nobody built it because dealing with banks was miserable. The schlep is the moat.

**How to surface schlep-blind ideas:**
- What do you do manually that should be automated?
- What do you put off because the existing solution is painful?
- What do successful companies rely on that still runs on spreadsheets, email, or phone calls?

---

### Do Things That Don't Scale → When to Scale

The transition from "do things that don't scale" to "build the machine" requires one signal: **the manual process is working and repeatable.**

You know it's working when:
- Users come back without prompting
- Users refer others without being asked
- You understand exactly why it works, not just that it does

Build the automation only when you can describe what the automation needs to do because you've done it manually dozens of times.

### The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups (Top 10 by Frequency)

| Mistake | What It Looks Like |
|---------|-------------------|
| **1. Single founder** | No peer to challenge bad decisions, share burden, or maintain momentum through adversity |
| **2. Bad location** | Away from ecosystem, talent, and investors who pattern-match on your space |
| **3. Marginal niche** | Small market with no growth trajectory — even 100% share is insufficient |
| **4. Derivative idea** | Copying what's working without finding the non-obvious improvement |
| **5. Obstinacy** | Refusing to change in response to feedback — confusing persistence with inflexibility |
| **6. Hiring bad people** | One B player brings in C players. Standards decay exponentially. |
| **7. Choosing the wrong platform** | Betting on a dying technology or building where you can't control the outcome |
| **8. Slow launch** | Spending 18 months building before showing anyone — the market's opinion costs nothing, your time costs everything |
| **9. Launching too early** | Opposite failure — releasing something unfinished to the wrong audience that forms a permanent negative impression |
| **10. No specific user in mind** | Building for "everyone" produces something excellent for no one |

### Maker vs. Manager Schedule

**Manager's schedule:** Calendar divided into hours. A meeting at 11am disrupts 10am and 11:30am. This is the operating mode of most organisations.

**Maker's schedule:** Calendar divided into half-days minimum. A single meeting in the middle of an afternoon destroys the entire afternoon of productive work. This is the operating mode of engineers, writers, and builders.

**The conflict:** Managers default to imposing their schedule on makers. A 30-minute meeting has zero cost on a manager schedule. It has a 3–4 hour cost on a maker schedule (setup time + the meeting + re-entry into deep work).

**Resolution rule:**
- Default to async for anything that doesn't require real-time agreement
- Batch all meetings to one day or one time block per day
- Protect uninterrupted blocks explicitly — they require defense, not polite requests
- Never schedule a single meeting in the middle of a maker's work block

## David Ogilvy — Advertising Principles

### The Foundational Premise

> "The consumer is not a moron. She is your wife."

Write advertising as if you are writing to an intelligent adult who has many demands on their attention. Condescension is the fastest path to irrelevance. Respect produces response.

---

### The 38 Ogilvy Principles (Top 12 for Direct Application)

1. **Headlines are 80% of the investment.** Five times as many people read the headline as read the body copy. If the headline fails, 80% of the money is wasted regardless of how good the copy is.

2. **Include the brand name in the headline.** Readers who don't read past the headline should still know who ran the ad.

3. **Specificity beats superlative.** "47 years of experience" beats "decades of expertise." "3 out of 4 dentists recommend" beats "preferred by dental professionals."

4. **Long copy outperforms short copy for considered purchases.** If the decision requires thought, more information produces more sales. Short copy signals a weak case.

5. **Testimonials and case studies outperform product descriptions.** What customers say about you is worth 10× what you say about yourself.

6. **Headlines with news outperform headlines without.** "New" and "Introducing" are among the highest-converting words. People buy what's new.

7. **Never use abstract illustration when photography is available.** Photographs of real products and real people outperform illustrations consistently.

8. **The "reason why" is the most powerful copy device.** Telling people WHY you're offering something removes skepticism. "We're overstocked, so we're cutting prices" outperforms "sale."

9. **Research before writing.** The more you know about a product, the more angles you find for attacking the market. Ogilvy spent months on research before writing a word on Rolls-Royce.

10. **Positioning is the most important decision.** Before deciding what to say, decide WHERE the product lives in the consumer's mind. The position determines everything that follows.

11. **Brand image compounds.** Every advertisement contributes to the long-term perception of the brand. Short-term promotions that degrade brand image are a loan against future revenue.

12. **Test everything.** Headlines, offers, formats, lengths. The market is the only judge. Personal preference is irrelevant. The response rate is the answer.

### The Big Idea

Ogilvy's singular obsession: advertising without a Big Idea is invisible.

> "Unless your advertising contains a big idea, it will pass like a ship in the night. I doubt if more than one campaign in a hundred contains a big idea."

**How to recognise a Big Idea:**
- Did it make you gasp when you first saw it?
- Do you wish you had thought of it yourself?
- Is it unique?
- Does it fit the brand strategy perfectly?
- Could it be used for 30 years?

**The Rolls-Royce headline (Big Idea example):**
*"At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock."*

This is a Big Idea because it communicates the entire brand position (engineering perfection, silence, luxury) in a single concrete, specific, verifiable fact. No adjectives. No claims. One fact that does all the work.

**Application:** Before writing any marketing copy, ask: "What is the single fact, observation, or demonstration that would make someone who is skeptical stop and say — 'I never thought of it that way'?" That is the Big Idea. Find it before you write anything else.

---

### Positioning

> "Every product has a natural and unique position in the consumer's mind. You can't impose a position from outside. You can only help the consumer see the position that already exists."

Before writing a word of copy, answer:
1. What does the customer believe about this category now?
2. What belief would need to change for them to value this product?
3. What is the single most credible, specific way to change that belief?

**Positioning errors:**
- Claiming a position already owned by a competitor (generic "quality" and "reliability")
- Claiming too many positions simultaneously (you can only own one thing)
- Choosing a position that the product doesn't actually deliver (position = promise, not aspiration)

---

### Research and Creative

Ogilvy's research method: read everything about the product and its category. Spend weeks if necessary. Write a 20-page brief before writing an ad. The Big Idea emerges from deep knowledge, not from a brainstorm.

**Creative quality test:**
- Would this be interesting to someone who has no interest in buying the product?
- Could someone describe this ad accurately to a friend in one sentence?
- Would this ad work without the logo (if yes, the Big Idea is strong enough; if no, the logo is carrying the load)?
