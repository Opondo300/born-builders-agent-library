# Branding — The Science of Meaning, Memory, and Market Position

Sources: McClure et al. (2004, Neuron), Deppe et al. (2005), Plassmann et al. (2008, PNAS/Stanford), Damasio, Zak et al. (2021, PubMed 34847200), Byron Sharp (How Brands Grow), Jenni Romaniuk (Building Distinctive Brand Assets), Aaker (Managing Brand Equity), Keller (Strategic Brand Management), Kapferer (The New Strategic Brand Management), Ries & Trout (Positioning), Neumeier (The Brand Gap), Mark & Pearson (The Hero and the Outlaw), Olins (Corporate Identity), Henderson & Cote (1998, JMR), Labrecque & Milne (2012, JAMS), CEB/Google "From Promotion to Emotion" (2013), Binet & Field (The Long and the Short of It)

## Part 1 — The Neuroscience of Brand Preference

### How Brands Modify the Brain's Reward System

**McClure et al. (2004, Neuron) — The Coke/Pepsi Study**

In blind taste tests: 50/50 preference. When participants knew they were drinking Coke: Coke preference dramatically increased. Knowing the brand activated the hippocampus (memory), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (expectation), and midbrain dopamine areas. These were not activated in the blind condition.

**The finding:** The brand doesn't just change what people say. It changes their actual neurological experience. The brain constructs subjective experience from expectation — and brand knowledge is the primary source of expectation.

**Practical implication:** A strong brand literally makes the product taste/feel/work better, subjectively and measurably, even when the product is identical to an unbranded alternative.

---

**Deppe et al. (2005) — Preferred Brands Deactivate Rational Processing**

When preferred brand was present:
- **vmPFC** (emotional reward) activated
- **dlPFC** (rational analysis) **deactivated**
- Decision time: **1,546ms** (preferred brand) vs **2,370ms** (non-preferred) — p < 0.000001

**The finding:** Preferred brands bypass analytical deliberation. The brain outsources the decision to the emotional reward system. Decision speed increases 35% because the analytical system has been circumvented.

**Practical implication:** A well-built brand doesn't just win comparisons — it eliminates the comparison.

---

**Plassmann et al. (2008, PNAS/Stanford/Caltech) — Price as Expectation Signal**

Higher price labels produced greater mOFC (pleasantness circuit) activation AND higher self-reported enjoyment — for identical wine.

**The finding:** Price modulates the mOFC before consumption — it changes predicted pleasure, which changes actual neurological experience.

**Practical implication:** Discounting a premium brand doesn't just cut margin; it reduces the neurological pleasure the product delivers.

---

**Zak et al. (2021, PubMed 34847200) — Competence Drives Trust**

Oxytocin increased brand competence perception by **132% vs. placebo**.

**Critical finding:** Trust mechanism in branding is driven primarily by **competence**, not warmth. Warmer is likeable; competent is trusted. Case studies with specific results, expert credentials, and demonstrated outcomes build oxytocin-trust more efficiently than approachable tone alone.

---

### Processing Fluency — Familiarity as Genuine Pleasure

Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman (2004): Processing fluency produces genuine positive affect. Easy-to-process stimuli activated the zygomaticus major (smiling muscle) before conscious evaluation.

**Zajonc's Mere Exposure Effect (1968/2001):** Repeated exposure increases liking, independent of quality. Optimal for initial preference: approximately 10–20 exposures.

**The practical rule:** Presence and repetition build preference through fluency alone — separate from quality signals.

## Part 2 — How Brands Actually Grow: The Ehrenberg-Bass Empirical Laws

### The Double Jeopardy Law

Smaller brands have **two disadvantages simultaneously** — fewer buyers AND slightly lower purchase frequency. This applies across all FMCG categories studied (100+ categories, 50 countries).

**The growth implication:** Growing a brand means primarily growing penetration (more buyers), not loyalty (more purchases from existing buyers). This is empirically false for virtually all categories studied.

**The loyalty myth:** Consumers buy from a repertoire. Sole buyers are a small minority.

---

### Mental and Physical Availability

**Mental availability:** The probability that a brand is noticed or thought of in buying situations. Built by breadth of memory links, freshness of memory, and quality of distinctive assets.

**Physical availability:** The ease with which a brand can be found and bought.

**The threshold effect:** ~80%+ distribution is a necessary (not sufficient) condition for brand growth. Mental availability is wasted if the consumer can't find the brand.

---

### The 11 Empirical Laws of Brand Growth

| Law | Summary |
|-----|---------|
| **Double Jeopardy** | Smaller brands have fewer buyers AND lower loyalty |
| **Retention Double Jeopardy** | Brands with fewer buyers also lose proportionally more |
| **Pareto Law Variation** | Heavy buyers are not quite as dominant as assumed (60/20 correction) |
| **Natural Monopoly** | Brands with more buyers attract more light buyers proportionally |
| **Law of Buyer Moderation** | Heavy buyers in one period are lighter in the next |
| **Duplication of Purchase** | Brands share customers in proportion to their size |
| **User Profiles** | Users of competing brands have similar profiles |
| **NBD-Dirichlet** | Purchase patterns predicted without loyalty parameter |
| **Attitudes Reflect Behavior** | Attitude differences mostly due to brand experience, not prior attitude |
| **Loyalty is Polygamous** | Most buyers buy from several competing brands |
| **Physical Availability Threshold** | Growth requires broad availability (~80%+ distribution) |

## Part 3 — Distinctive Assets

### The Romaniuk Framework

Distinctiveness = being recognizable as yourself. Differentiation = being meaningfully different. Sharp: 77% of Apple buyers don't perceive Apple as meaningfully different on functional attributes. They buy because Apple is mentally and physically available.

**"Customers don't care if you have a USP. They care whether they can think of you and find you."**

### The Distinctive Asset Grid

Two dimensions:
- **Fame axis (y-axis):** % of category buyers who correctly attribute the asset to the brand
- **Uniqueness axis (x-axis):** % who attribute the asset ONLY to this brand

| Quadrant | Fame | Uniqueness | Strategy |
|----------|------|-----------|---------|
| **Star** | High | High | Protect, never change |
| **Vulnerable** | High | Low | Increase uniqueness or accept shared ownership |
| **Emerging** | Low | High | Invest to increase fame |
| **Weak** | Low | Low | Retire or significantly invest |

**Empirical benchmarks:** Only 4% of colors and 6% of taglines achieve strong distinctiveness.

### Category Entry Points (CEPs)

CEPs are memories, thoughts, feelings, and contexts that precede or coincide with a buying situation.

**The 7W framework:**
| Dimension | Question |
|-----------|---------|
| **When** | At what times does this category come to mind? |
| **Where** | In what locations? |
| **With/for whom** | Who else is involved? |
| **How feeling** | What emotional state precedes the purchase? |
| **While** | What else is being done simultaneously? |
| **With what** | What other products is this used alongside? |
| **Why** | What goal or motivation drives the purchase? |

**Strategic objective:** Own as many CEPs as possible. Narrow positioning (owning one CEP deeply) is strategically inferior to broad positioning.

## Part 4 — Brand Identity Frameworks

### Aaker's Brand Identity System

**Core Identity (2–4 associations):** Timeless essence. Remains constant across new markets, products, and visual refreshes. Must be unique, resonant, durable, and deliverable.

**The Four Brand Perspective Quadrants:**
| Perspective | Key Question |
|-------------|-------------|
| **Brand as Product** | What does this product do and for whom? |
| **Brand as Organization** | What does the company stand for? |
| **Brand as Person** | If this brand were a person, who would it be? |
| **Brand as Symbol** | What visual and symbolic associations define this brand? |

**Five Brand Personality Dimensions:**
| Dimension | Traits | Examples |
|-----------|--------|---------|
| **Sincerity** | Down-to-earth, honest, wholesome | Hallmark, Campbell's |
| **Excitement** | Daring, spirited, imaginative | Mountain Dew, Red Bull |
| **Competence** | Reliable, intelligent, successful | IBM, Wall Street Journal |
| **Sophistication** | Upper class, charming | Mercedes-Benz, Gucci |
| **Ruggedness** | Outdoorsy, tough | Jeep, Levi's |

**Five Brand Equity Dimensions:**
| Dimension | Value Created |
|-----------|--------------|
| **Brand Awareness** | Anchors associations; creates familiarity; drives consideration |
| **Brand Associations** | Differentiates; provides reason to buy |
| **Perceived Quality** | Justifies premium pricing |
| **Brand Loyalty** | Reduces marketing costs; provides competitive resilience |
| **Proprietary Assets** | Prevents imitation |

### Keller's CBBE Pyramid — The Resonance Model

```
              [4. RESONANCE]
        [3a. Judgments] [3b. Feelings]
    [2a. Performance] [2b. Imagery]
              [1. SALIENCE]
```

**Level 1 — Salience:** Depth (how easily comes to mind) + Breadth (how many situations trigger recall).

**Level 2 — Meaning:**
- *Performance* (rational route): Primary ingredients, reliability, serviceability, style, price
- *Imagery* (emotional route): User profiles, usage situations, brand personality, heritage

**Level 3 — Response:**
- *Judgments*: Quality, credibility, consideration, superiority
- *Feelings* (six types, from immediate to enduring):
  1. Warmth · 2. Fun · 3. Excitement · 4. Security · 5. Social approval · 6. Self-respect

**Level 4 — Resonance (apex):**
1. Behavioral loyalty — consistent repeat purchase
2. Attitudinal attachment — "This is my brand."
3. Sense of community
4. Active engagement — investment beyond purchase

**The Two Routes to Resonance:**
- Left (rational): Salience → Performance → Judgments → Resonance
- Right (emotional): Salience → Imagery → Feelings → Resonance

### Kapferer's Brand Identity Prism

Six facets across two axes (externalization/internalization; sender/receiver):

| Facet | Side | Key Question |
|-------|------|-------------|
| **Physique** | External | What does this brand look like? What is its characteristic form? |
| **Personality** | External | How does this brand communicate? What tone? What character? |
| **Culture** | External-Internal | What does this brand fundamentally stand for? |
| **Relationship** | External | What does the brand DO for/with the customer? |
| **Reflection** | Internal | Who does the brand present as its customer? |
| **Self-image** | Internal | "When I use this brand, I am the kind of person who..." |

**Crucial distinction:** Reflection = how others see you (external). Self-image = how you see yourself (internal). Brands operating on Self-image achieve the deepest customer attachment.

## Part 5 — Positioning

### Ries & Trout's Foundational Principles

**"Positioning is not what you do to a product. It is what you do to the mind of the prospect."**

### The 7±2 Mental Ladder

Consumers hold approximately 7 brands in active memory per category, with effective range 3–5. The top rung receives 2–4x more consideration than position 2.

**The implication:** If you are not in the top 3, you are fighting for scraps.

### First vs. Better

Better to be first than to be better. Being first means:
1. The brand often becomes the generic name for the category
2. First brand is the benchmark against which all others are measured negatively
3. Memory associations formed first are stronger and more resistant to revision

**Rule for late entrants:** Find an unclaimed position (a dimension the pioneer hasn't owned) or create a new ladder where you are first.

### Neumeier — The ONLINESS Statement

**"[Brand X] is the only [category] that [differentiator] for [customer] in [context] who [customer need/tension]."**

**Test:** Can you state your differentiation without comparing yourself to a competitor? If you need "unlike X, we..." — you are incrementally different. If you can state it without any competitor reference — you are radically different.

## Part 6 — Brand Archetypes

Mark & Pearson's 12 archetypes based on Jungian psychology:

| Archetype | Core Desire | Fear | Brand Examples |
|-----------|------------|------|---------------|
| **Innocent** | Paradise, simplicity | Punishment | Dove, Coca-Cola (classic) |
| **Explorer** | Self-discovery | Conformity | Jeep, Patagonia, REI |
| **Sage** | Truth | Ignorance | Harvard, BBC, Google |
| **Hero** | Prove worth | Weakness | Nike, FedEx, BMW |
| **Outlaw** | Revolution | Powerlessness | Harley-Davidson, Virgin |
| **Magician** | Transformation | Unintended consequences | Apple, Disney |
| **Everyman** | Belonging | Elitism | IKEA, Target, Ford |
| **Lover** | Intimacy | Being unwanted | Chanel, Häagen-Dazs |
| **Jester** | Enjoyment | Boredom | M&Ms, Old Spice |
| **Caregiver** | Protect others | Selfishness | Johnson & Johnson, Volvo |
| **Creator** | Create enduring value | Mediocrity | Lego, Adobe |
| **Ruler** | Control, order | Chaos | American Express, Rolex |

**The one-archetype rule:** Brands trying to embody multiple archetypes produce confused signals.

## Part 7 — Brand Architecture

### The Three Olins Models

**Monolithic / Branded House:** One identity across everything. Examples: Virgin, BMW, FedEx.
- Best when: Parent brand is strong and associations transfer positively to all businesses

**Endorsed Brand:** Parent endorses sub-brands, lending credibility. Examples: Marriott/Ritz-Carlton, Nestlé/KitKat.
- Best when: Sub-brands need distinct identities but benefit from parent credibility

**House of Brands / Freestanding:** Parent corporation is essentially invisible. Examples: P&G (Tide, Pampers), Unilever (Dove, Axe).
- Best when: Brands serve fundamentally different audiences with conflicting associations

## Part 8 — Visual Identity Science

### Logo Complexity and Recognition

Henderson & Cote (1998, JMR): Highest-recognition logos are "very natural, very harmonious, moderately elaborate."

**Shape complexity and perceived personality:**
- Simple logos → warmth perception
- Complex logos → competence perception

**Angular vs. round shapes:**
- Angular logos → premium, competence, masculinity
- Round/curved logos → warmth, communal, approachable

### Color Psychology

Labrecque & Milne (2012, JAMS) — color-brand personality congruence:
| Brand Personality Dimension | Associated Colors |
|---------------------------|-----------------|
| **Sincerity** | Warm whites, warm pastels |
| **Excitement** | Red, orange, electric yellow |
| **Competence** | Blue (especially dark/deep blue) |
| **Sophistication** | Black, dark navy, jewel tones |
| **Ruggedness** | Brown, earthy greens |

**The golden rule:** Own one color before claiming two. Color ownership is a valuable distinctive asset.

## Part 9 — B2B Brand Mechanics

### Personal Value Is the Decisive Variable

CEB/Google "From Promotion to Emotion" (2013) — n=3,000 B2B buyers:

B2B buyers who perceived **personal value** (career advancement, risk reduction, peer respect) were:
- **50% more likely to buy** than those perceiving only business value
- **8× more likely to pay a premium**
- **71% willing to pay a premium** when personal value was perceived, vs. **12%** for business value alone

**The critical insight:** The "rational" B2B purchase is driven by the buyer's personal situation — their status, risk exposure, career trajectory.

### The 46/54 Brand-to-Activation Ratio

LinkedIn B2B Institute / Binet & Field: Optimal allocation is approximately **46% brand building / 54% activation**.

**Why brand investment matters in B2B:**
- Only 5–10% of B2B buyers are in active purchase mode at any time
- Brand investment reaches the 90–95% who are not yet buying — but will be
- **86% of enterprise buyers start research with brands already known to them**
- **81% win rate** for the best-known brand in competitive evaluation

### Thought Leadership as Trust Currency

Edelman-LinkedIn B2B Thought Leadership Impact Study (2024):
- **75%** of senior decision-makers research new products primarily through thought leadership
- **9 in 10** decision-makers more receptive to sales outreach from brands that publish high-quality thought leadership

## Part 10 — Brand Naming Science

### The Bouba/Kiki Effect

Ramachandran & Hubbard (2001): ~95–98% of participants mapped "kiki" to spiky shapes and "bouba" to rounded shapes. Validated across 25 languages, 9 language families.

**The naming implication:** A name's sound carries meaning before it means anything.

| Sound Pattern | Signals | Use When |
|---------------|---------|----------|
| Sharp phonemes (/k/, /t/, /p/) | Sharpness, precision, tension | Technology, performance brands |
| Round phonemes (/m/, /l/, /b/, /o/) | Warmth, softness, accessibility | Food, care, comfort brands |

### Arbitrary vs. Descriptive Names

**Why arbitrary names win long-term:**
- Descriptive names are pre-loaded with category associations that constrain brand meaning
- Arbitrary names have no prior associations — they become whatever associations the brand builds
- Arbitrary names are more distinctive and more protectable legally

### Optimal Name Structure

**Length:** 6–8 characters, 1–3 syllables. Fast to process, easy to remember, easy to say.

**The Law of Three Syllables:** Names with more than three syllables get shortened by users. Design the short version intentionally.

## Brand Diagnostic

**Identity:**
1. Can you complete the ONLINESS statement?
2. Have all six Kapferer prism facets been defined?
3. Is the archetype clear? Can every team member articulate it?
4. Is core identity documented in 2–4 associations?

**Position:**
5. What mental ladder does this brand occupy? What rung?
6. Is the position based on something structurally hard to copy?
7. Have you found a new category to be first in?

**Equity:**
8. Where is the brand on the CBBE pyramid?
9. What is the brand's position on the Distinctive Asset Grid for its top 3 assets?

**Growth:**
10. Is growth strategy penetration-focused (Ehrenberg-Bass) or retention-focused?
11. How many Category Entry Points does the brand own?
12. Is physical availability above 80% threshold?

**B2B (when relevant):**
13. Does the brand communicate personal value, not just organizational value?
14. Does brand investment reach buyers who are not currently in purchase mode?
15. Is thought leadership creating CEP linkage during the research phase?
