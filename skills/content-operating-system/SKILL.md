# Content Operating System — The Science of What Spreads and Why

Sources: Berger & Milkman (2012 NYT study), Jonah Berger (STEPPS/Contagious), Duncan Watts (MusicLab), Kurt Vonnegut (story shapes), Reagan et al. (2016 computational analysis), Robert McKee (Story), Donald Miller (StoryBrand), Matthew Dicks (Storyworthy), Uri Hasson (neural coupling), Paul Zak (oxytocin/narrative), Seth Godin (Tribes/This Is Marketing), Kevin Kelly (1,000 True Fans), Transport Theory (Green & Brock, 2000), Paul Slovic (psychic numbing), Kogut & Ritov (singularity effect), Jonathan Haidt (moral elevation), Hatfield/Cacioppo/Rapson (emotional contagion), Fowler & Christakis (2008), Horton & Wohl (1956 parasocial), Zajonc (mere exposure), Carlson & Shu (Rule of Three), Aronson (pratfall effect), Dan McAdams (narrative identity), Nyhan & Reifler (backfire effect)

## The Foundational Distinction

"Viral" and "distributed" are not the same thing. Duncan Watts' MusicLab study (2006) showed which songs became hits diverged substantially between identical groups depending solely on early social momentum — not content quality. Most "viral" content is heavily distributed content. The content's properties did not cause the spread.

You cannot reliably engineer a viral hit. You can engineer content with a high probability of spreading — and separately engineer distribution that amplifies it. These are different skills.

## Part 1 — The Science of Sharing

### Berger & Milkman NYT Study (2012) — The Most Important Virality Research

~7,000 New York Times articles. What made the "most emailed" list?

**Central finding:** The variable that predicted sharing was not positive vs. negative. It was **arousal level** — physiological activation.

| Emotion | Arousal | Virality Effect |
|---------|---------|----------------|
| Awe | High (positive) | Strongly viral |
| Anger | High (negative) | Strongly viral |
| Anxiety | High (negative) | Strongly viral |
| Joy/Amusement | High (positive) | Viral |
| Moral Elevation | High (positive) | Strongly viral + prosocial |
| Sadness | Low (deactivating) | Below baseline |
| Contentment | Low (positive) | Below baseline |

**The mechanism:** High-arousal emotions activate the sympathetic nervous system. This creates an urge to act. Sharing channels the arousal into social connection. Sadness suppresses the urge to share.

**Most reliable positive emotion for sharing: Awe.** High-arousal, prosocial, no reputational risk. Triggers: scale, complexity, excellence, unexpected beauty, moral elevation, revelation that changes understanding.

---

### Berger's STEPPS Framework — 6 Drivers of Social Transmission

**S — Social Currency**
People share things that make them look good: smart, in-the-know, interesting. Inner remarkability (counterintuitive information), game mechanics (achievement/status), insider access. If sharing something makes the sharer look better, it will be shared.

**T — Triggers**
Products frequently triggered by the environment get talked about more than interesting products that appear rarely. What environmental cues trigger your content category?

**E — Emotion**
When we care, we share. High-arousal emotions drive sharing more than any other variable.

**P — Public**
Observable behavior is imitable behavior. Content that displays publicly creates social proof that amplifies further sharing.

**P — Practical Value**
News you can use. People share useful content because it helps the recipient AND makes the sharer look knowledgeable. Overlap of usefulness and social value = highest-sharing practical content.

**S — Stories**
Information travels wrapped in narrative. The message must be so embedded in the story that extracting it requires taking the story with it.

---

### Viral Coefficient (K-Factor)

```
K = i × c
i = average invites/shares per user
c = conversion rate of those invites
```

- K > 1: Exponential growth
- K < 1: Requires external acquisition (most B2B products operate K = 0.3–0.7)

**The underappreciated variable — cycle time:** K = 1.2 with 30-day cycles produces dramatically slower growth than K = 1.2 with 3-day cycles.

## Part 2 — Storytelling: The Deep Craft

### Neuroscience Foundation

**Uri Hasson's Neural Coupling (Princeton):** When a person tells a story, listeners' brains synchronize with the storyteller's. In high-coupling conditions, the listener's brain patterns can precede the speaker's — the listener is completing the story. When you tell a story your audience has experienced, their brain is not processing your story. It is being their story.

**Paul Zak — Oxytocin and Narrative:** Character-driven stories with tension cause oxytocin release. Conditions required:
1. A character the audience can identify with
2. That character faces specific, concrete tension
3. Stakes are clear (what if unresolved?)
4. The character shows agency — they try to solve the problem
5. Resolution comes with cost — something was sacrificed

**Transportation Theory (Green & Brock, 2000):** When readers are transported (mentally absorbed into narrative), they lower their analytical guard. Counter-arguing is suppressed during transportation. Transported audiences remember content far longer than equivalent factual information.

---

### The 5-Second Moment (Matthew Dicks — 52× Moth StorySLAM champion)

Every great story is built around a single moment — often 1–5 seconds — in which the protagonist's understanding permanently changes.

**The purpose of every great story is to bring a singular moment of transformation to the greatest clarity possible.**

**Structure:**
1. Find the 5-second moment first
2. Identify its opposite — the prior state
3. Open the story at or near that opposite state
4. Everything exists to make the transformation moment land with maximum clarity

**What it looks like:** The realization you were wrong about something important. The moment you understood what you actually valued. The moment failure became the teacher. The moment you saw what you'd been missing.

**What it does NOT look like:** A sequence of events. A series of achievements. An anecdote. A story requires transformation.

### Vonnegut Story Shapes — 8 Emotional Architectures

Validated by Reagan et al. (2016) — computational analysis of 1,737 fiction works.

| Shape | Arc | Use When |
|-------|-----|----------|
| **Man in a Hole** | ↓↑ | Case studies, recovery stories, most B2B narratives |
| **Boy Meets Girl** | ↑↓↑ | Relationship-to-brand stories |
| **Rags to Riches** | ↑↑↑ | Founder stories, origin myths |
| **Icarus** | ↑↑↓ | Cautionary tales, competitor failure |
| **Oedipus** | ↓↑↓↓ | Tragedy with moral lesson |
| **Cinderella** | →↑↑↑ | Transformation marketing |
| **New Testament** | ↓↓↓↑↑↑ | Strongest emotional arc; brand mythology |
| **Hamlet** | ↕↕↕? | Philosophical, invites reflection |

**Most resonant for marketing:** "Man in a Hole" — mirrors the buyer's journey. "New Testament" — strongest emotional arc for brand mythology.

### McKee Gap — The Three-Level Problem Structure

| Level | What It Is | Example | Impact |
|-------|-----------|---------|--------|
| **External** | Physical, tangible problem | "Can't generate referrals" | Low emotional resonance |
| **Internal** | Emotional/identity problem | "I feel like I'm falling behind everyone else" | High resonance — the real buyer |
| **Philosophical** | The wrongness of the problem | "A good provider shouldn't have to do this manually" | Creates moral urgency |

**Rule:** Identify the internal problem first. The external is the surface. The internal is what the buyer is actually paying to escape.

### The Pixar Story Spine — Causal Chain Template

```
Once upon a time ___
Every day ___
But one day ___
Because of that ___
Because of that ___
Because of that ___
Until finally ___
And ever since then ___
```

The repeated "because of that" forces explicit causal linkage. The hippocampus is specifically wired to process causal sequences. Use to structure any case study or transformation narrative.

---

### The Hero as Customer (SB7 — Donald Miller)

The brand is never the hero. The brand is the guide. Positioning the brand as the hero removes the customer from the story.

1. **Character** — What does the customer want? One specific desire.
2. **Problem** — Three levels (external, internal, philosophical)
3. **Guide** — Empathy (I understand) + Authority (I've helped others)
4. **Plan** — 3 steps maximum
5. **Call to Action** — Direct or transitional
6. **Failure** — What happens if they don't act?
7. **Success** — Specific, vivid, sensory vision of the outcome

## Part 3 — Hook Engineering

### The Neurological Mechanism

The brain runs on prediction. A hook creates a **prediction error** — the brain encounters something that doesn't match its model. This triggers:
1. Orienting response (limbic novelty/threat detection)
2. Dopamine release (anticipation of resolving the error)
3. Shift from automatic to deliberate processing

**Four categories of prediction errors:**
1. **Threat** — "You're losing $X every month you do this"
2. **Novelty** — Information the brain has no existing schema for
3. **High-arousal emotion** — Awe, anger, anxiety
4. **Curiosity gap** — Partial information that creates an unresolved loop

### Headline Data

- 70–80% of readers read only the headline (Chartbeat, Penn State 2024, Ogilvy)
- Ogilvy: "Five times as many people read the headline as read the body copy. When you have written your headline, you have spent eighty cents out of your dollar."
- For 70–80% of people, the headline is the entire impression

### Platform-Specific Hook Data

**LinkedIn:**
- "See More" cutoff: ~220 characters
- Top-performing hook types: "How I..." (personal experience), "How to..." (promise), surprising statistic, story opening
- First line must complete a thought AND create a curiosity gap

**Email subject lines:**
- 64% of recipients make open decision on subject line alone
- Question-framed: 46% open rate vs 35% generic (+31%)
- Under 70 characters achieves highest open rates (mobile)
- Urgency language boosts +22%

**YouTube:**
- CTR benchmarks: <3% = fix; 4–6% = average; 7–10% = good; >10% = excellent
- Front-load titles with hook in first 40 characters (mobile cutoff)

**The F-pattern (Nielsen NNG):**
- First: Full horizontal sweep across the top (headline)
- Second: Shorter horizontal sweep lower (first sentence)
- Third: Vertical scan down the left side

**Implication:** Place most important information in first 2 lines. First few words of every paragraph must carry the point.

## Part 4 — The Identifiable Victim Effect

This is the most counterintuitive finding in content persuasion. The research is unambiguous.

### The Core Research

**Paul Slovic — "Psychic Numbing and Genocide" (2007)**

- Emotional response is greatest at N=1
- Begins to diminish at N=2
- Large numbers: emotional flatline

The empathy system (medial prefrontal cortex) is optimized for perspective-taking with one identified individual. It cannot maintain perspective across multiple people.

**Small, Loewenstein & Slovic (2007) — The Paradox of Adding Statistics**

When researchers told participants about an identifiable victim AND provided statistics about the larger problem, charitable giving DROPPED compared to telling them about the individual only.

**The mechanism:** Statistics trigger deliberate analytical thinking, which disrupts the affect heuristic. Analytical processing suppresses the emotional response that drives action.

**The content implication: adding data to a personal story reduces its persuasive power.**

**Kogut & Ritov (2005) — The Singularity Effect**

Being the only one matters more than being named. A single identified victim elicits considerably more contributions than identified members of a group.

### Content Creation Rules

1. **Name the one person.** Age, location, specific detail. Not "an NDIS provider" — "Michael, who runs 6 SIL homes in Parramatta."
2. **Never add statistics alongside the individual story.** Use data in a separate piece.
3. **One, not "one of many."** "One of 50,000 providers affected" reintroduces scale → numbing.
4. **The singularity is the mechanism.** Being alone in their situation triggers disproportionate empathy.
5. **The named villain gets the same effect.** One specific named practice as antagonist drives more engagement than "the industry."

## Part 5 — Emotional Contagion and Moral Elevation

### Emotional Contagion — The Mechanism

**Hatfield, Cacioppo & Rapson (1993):** People automatically mimic and synchronize expressions with others and, as a consequence, converge emotionally.

**The three-step mechanism:**
1. The body mimics the emotional expression it observes
2. The mimicked expression generates actual emotional state change in the observer
3. The observer begins to experience the observed emotion

This is not cognitive — it is automatic. Authentic emotional expression triggers genuine contagion. Manufactured enthusiasm is detected because it lacks the specific micro-expression signatures that trigger contagion.

**Fowler & Christakis (2008):** Happiness spreads through social networks up to three degrees of separation.

**Contagion velocity by type:**
- Anger: fastest and furthest (divisive audience)
- Awe: nearly as far, net-positive audience quality
- Joy: spreads but decays quickly
- Sadness: deactivating — reduces sharing impulse

---

### Moral Elevation — Haidt's Discovery (2003)

**Definition:** The warm, uplifting feeling when witnessing unexpected human virtue, moral beauty, or exceptional human goodness.

**Physiological signature:**
- Warm, pleasant feeling in the chest
- Sometimes tears (not of sadness)
- Felt desire to become a better person
- Felt desire to perform acts of kindness
- Sense of love and connection with humanity

**Schnall & Roper (2012):** Elevation specifically increased altruistic behavior compared to control conditions.

**The moral elevation content formula:**
1. Establish the difficulty or ethical pressure the protagonist faces
2. Show the easier/worse path clearly (the temptation)
3. Show the protagonist choosing integrity at cost
4. Show the cost — what they gave up or risked
5. Show the downstream effect (optional but amplifying)

**Strategic comparison:**

| Metric | Anger | Awe | Moral Elevation |
|--------|-------|-----|----------------|
| Sharing rate | Highest | High | High |
| Audience quality | Divisive | Neutral | Prosocial |
| Creator risk | High | Low | Very low |
| Downstream behavior | Outrage | Wonder | Action + generosity |

## Part 6 — Parasocial Relationship Mechanics

### The Original Research (Horton & Wohl, 1956)

**Parasocial interaction (PSI):** The experience of intimacy within a single viewing/reading episode — fleeting.

**Parasocial relationship (PSR):** The accumulated bond that develops across multiple encounters — the asset.

Most content creators understand PSI but do not systematically build PSR.

### Five Behaviors That Build Parasocial Bonds

1. **Direct address** — Speak to "you," not "my audience." Look directly into camera. Name the specific type of person you're speaking to.
2. **Controlled self-disclosure** — Consistent, specific, genuine details of real experience including failure. One-off vulnerability = performance. Accumulated disclosure over time = friendship.
3. **Consistency of schedule and voice** — Showing up on a predictable cadence creates anticipation → relationship. Inconsistency cannot build PSR.
4. **Responsiveness** — Responding to comments by name, referencing audience questions. The audience member named once feels permanently seen.
5. **Behind-the-scenes access** — Process, not just product. The thinking in progress. The failed attempt.

### The Mere Exposure Effect (Zajonc, 1968)

Repeated exposure to a stimulus increases liking, independent of quality. Familiarity reduces processing disfluency; fluency is experienced as liking.

**Content implication:** A good post published consistently outperforms a great post published erratically. The algorithm rewards consistency, and mere exposure rewards frequency.

**Optimal exposures for initial preference:** 10–20. After that the effect plateaus and can reverse for low-quality content.

## Part 7 — Rhetorical Structures as Memory Engineering

### Rule of Three (Tricolon) — Carlson & Shu (2014)

Three positive claims is the optimal number for persuasion. A fourth claim triggers persuasion knowledge — the audience's awareness that they are being persuaded — and causes retrospective skepticism about all claims including the first three.

**The fourth claim paradox:** Adding a fourth benefit to an ad with three reduces persuasiveness even when the fourth benefit is real. Delete the fourth claim, do not improve it.

**Application:** Three key benefits. Three proof points. Three reasons. Three steps. Never four.

### Anaphora — The Repetition Structure

"I have a dream" (8 repetitions). Mechanism:
1. Anticipation effect — by the third repetition, audiences begin to anticipate the phrase
2. Emotional escalation — each repetition builds cumulative intensity (does not reset)
3. Memory encoding — phonological loop encodes rhythmic repetition more reliably
4. Neural coupling — enables the audience to co-produce the phrase

**Rule:** Minimum three repetitions. Two is a parallel. Three is anaphora.

### Chiasmus — The Reversal Structure

"Ask not what your country can do for you — ask what you can do for your country."

**Why it works:** The reversal triggers cognitive pattern-completion. The symmetry is experienced as completeness — "both sides have been considered." Creates double encoding: both content and form are memorable.

**Application:** "The problem isn't finding coordinators. The problem is that coordinators can't find you."

### Alliteration — Sound as Memory Tool

Phonetically similar sounds create cognitive links between concepts — connected by sound before meaning. The phonological loop rehearses alliterative sequences more efficiently.

**Application:** "Manual process. Maximum liability. Minimum margin."

### The Pratfall Effect (Aronson, 1966)

A highly competent person is rated MORE attractive after a minor mistake. The same mistake makes an average person LESS attractive.

**Condition:** The pratfall effect only works after credibility is established. Admitting weakness before establishing expertise reads as genuine weakness.

## Part 8 — Narrative Identity (McAdams)

People do not experience their lives as a sequence of events. They construct those events into a narrative — a story with a protagonist, antagonists, turning points, and an overarching theme.

When a piece of content touches a reader's self-narrative — confirming it, challenging it, or offering a better version — the engagement is qualitatively different.

**Redemption sequence:** A negative event leads to growth, transformation, positive meaning. Associated with well-being and prosocial behavior.

**Contamination sequence:** A positive situation is destroyed or corrupted. Associated with low well-being and disengagement.

**Content implication:** Audiences share redemption narratives. Contamination narratives (pure complaint, no resolution) get agreement but not deep engagement.

**The "written for me" mechanism:**

Content creates this experience when:
1. **Specificity triggers personal memory recall** — "A 43-year-old who runs 6 SIL homes in Parramatta" activates memory networks that "an NDIS provider" does not
2. **The narrative arc mirrors the reader's own story structure** — redemptive vs. contamination; progressive vs. regressive
3. **The character occupies the reader's self-concept or aspirational identity** — "That's exactly my situation" (current self) OR "That's who I want to become" (aspirational self)

**The practical test:** Would the target reader say "that's exactly my situation" or "that's who I want to become"? If neither: no self-narrative alignment.

## Part 9 — Platform-Specific Narrative Architecture

### LinkedIn — The Earned Authority Arc

1. Personal failure, cost, or counterintuitive observation (hook — curiosity gap + vulnerability)
2. The insight that changed how you see the problem (the reframe)
3. The mechanism — why it works this way (authority)
4. The implication — what the reader should do or think differently (value delivery)
5. A question that invites comment (algorithm signal)

**Critical mechanics:**
- Hook must complete a thought AND create curiosity gap within 220 characters
- Links in body suppress reach — post links in first comment
- Documents and carousels generate 3–5x more dwell time
- The Golden Hour (first 60–90 minutes) determines algorithmic amplification
- Reply to every early comment in the first hour

### YouTube — Cold Open and Revelation Gap

- **Cold open (0–5 seconds):** Show the most compelling moment from the video. Then reset.
- **The revelation gap:** Withhold the single most important insight until 70–80% through the video. Never put the best thing first.
- **Earn the next minute test:** Every 60 seconds must justify the next 60 seconds.

### TikTok — Dual Hook + Completion Rate

- **Visual hook (frame 1):** Something unexpected in the opening frame
- **Verbal hook (seconds 1–3):** Problem, promise, or counterintuitive claim. No preamble.
- **Completion rate threshold:** 70–75% = algorithm escalation to larger pools
- **Pattern interrupt at 30 seconds:** Location/visual/tone change at 25–30 second mark

### Instagram Carousel — Progressive Revelation

- Slide 1: Confrontational claim that demands resolution
- Slides 3–7: One insight per slide, progressive
- Slide 8: The revelation that reframes everything
- Final slide: A question (not a statement) as CTA
- Each slide must end with an incomplete thought — never a conclusion

### Email Newsletter — Sequential Contract

- Subject line: Opens a loop. Does not summarize. Creates a gap.
- Preview text: Deepens the loop — does not preview content
- Opening line: Pays off the subject line OR detonates a new, larger loop
- Body: One problem → specific insight → proof → implication → CTA
- **The one idea rule:** One idea per email. Multiple ideas teach readers to skim.

### Where People Leave (Attention Curves)

| Platform | Primary Drop-off | Prevention |
|----------|-----------------|------------|
| LinkedIn | After first line (220 chars) | Curiosity gap that requires continuation |
| YouTube | 0–30 seconds | Cold open + specific promise |
| TikTok | 10–15 seconds | Deliver value before second 15 |
| Instagram | Between slides 2–3 | Agitation must exceed slide 1 tension |
| Twitter threads | Between tweets 3–4 | Each tweet must advance AND be standalone |
| Email | After opening line | Pattern interrupt in the first sentence |

## Part 10 — The Villain in Content: Tribal Cohesion

**Henri Tajfel's Social Identity Theory (1979):** People categorize themselves into groups and derive self-esteem from those groups. They will irrationally favor their in-group even at personal cost.

**McKee's principle:** The antagonist defines the protagonist. Content that lacks a clear antagonist has no dramatic engine.

### Types of Villains — Hierarchy of Risk and Reward

| Villain Type | Engagement | Risk | Best For |
|-------------|------------|------|----------|
| **The status quo** | High | Very low | Establishing category |
| **The mindset** | High | Low | Positioning against competing solutions |
| **The system** | High | Low | Structural critique |
| **The behavior** | Very high | Medium | Industry improvement narrative |
| **The specific company** | Highest | High | Only with evidence; use rarely |

**The safest and most durable villain:** The outdated or broken system. No lawyers. No sympathy. Never files a complaint.

### Rules of the Villain

1. **Name the villain your audience already has.** You are not introducing — you are naming.
2. **The villain must be external to the audience.** Never make the prospect the villain.
3. **The audience is the hero, you are the guide.**
4. **Specific beats generic.** "Roster software built before SCHADS existed" beats "outdated scheduling systems."
5. **The villain creates urgency.** The status quo is costing the audience right now.

## Part 11 — The Counterintuitive Content Hierarchy

### Why Counterintuitive Works

The dopamine prediction error system fires when incoming information contradicts existing schema. The strength of the response is proportional to the magnitude of the prediction error. Confirmation of existing beliefs produces no dopamine spike. Violation produces the largest spike.

### Counterintuitive Content Formula (5-Part Structure)

1. **State the common belief** — the widely-held assumption
2. **Validate why people believe it** — give the belief its best argument (this is critical — it signals you're not attacking people for holding it)
3. **The counter-evidence** — what the data or experience actually shows
4. **The hidden mechanism** — why the counterintuitive truth is actually true
5. **The implication** — what this means the audience should do differently

### The Four Tiers

| Tier | Type | Engagement |
|------|------|------------|
| **Tier 1** | Inverts a confident consensus belief | Maximum — largest prediction error |
| **Tier 2** | Reveals the hidden mechanism behind a known outcome | High — expected explanation violated |
| **Tier 3** | Shows the unexpected cost of a widely recommended approach | High — trusted advice shown to have hidden cost |
| **Tier 4** | Confirms an emerging suspicion with evidence | Moderate — lower prediction error, high validation |

### Counterintuitive vs. Contrarian

| Type | Basis | Risk |
|------|-------|------|
| **Counterintuitive** | Evidence-based reality that differs from consensus | Low — evidence defends you |
| **Contrarian** | Arguing the opposite position | High — if evidence shifts, you're exposed |

Counterintuitive reveals the world works differently than people think. Contrarian argues they are wrong to think what they think. One creates curiosity; the other creates defensiveness.

### Avoiding the Backfire Effect (Nyhan & Reifler, 2010)

Triggered when: the challenge is direct, the evidence is framed as proving the person wrong, credibility isn't established first, or the challenged belief is tied to group identity.

**How to avoid:**
1. Challenge the information the person received, not the person who received it
2. Use questions rather than corrections
3. Build credibility on adjacent claims before challenging the core belief
4. Frame as a discovery you made, not a correction you're delivering

The counterintuitive formula naturally avoids the backfire effect because Step 2 (validating why people believe it) signals you understand and don't blame them.

## Seth Godin's Strategic Layer

### Minimum Viable Audience

> "The solution is to stake out the smallest market you can imagine — the smallest market that can sustain you, the smallest market you can adequately serve."

1,000 people who find your content essential beats 100,000 who mildly appreciate it. Serving everyone means being adequately relevant to all and essential to none.

**Kevin Kelly's 1,000 True Fans:** An independent creator needs ~1,000 true fans (people who buy everything) to sustain a viable creative career.

### Tribe Mechanics

A tribe requires: shared interest AND a mechanism for members to communicate with each other. Shared interest without communication = audience. Communication without shared interest = noise.

**What tribal leaders do:**
- Define who is NOT in the tribe (exclusion is as important as inclusion)
- Provide a clear enemy: a status quo, a mindset
- Connect tribe members to each other — not just to the leader
- Do NOT try to please everyone — watering down destroys the tribe

## Content Diagnostic (17 Points)

Before producing any content:

1. **Arousal level:** Which emotion? Is it high-arousal? (awe / anger / anxiety / joy / moral elevation)
2. **STEPPS:** Which of the 6 elements apply?
3. **Story level:** Is there a transformation moment? What is the 5-second realization? Is the arc redemptive?
4. **McKee's Gap:** What is the gap between expectation and reality?
5. **Problem level:** Are you addressing the internal problem, not just external?
6. **Hook:** What prediction error does the first line create? Which of the 4 categories?
7. **Format:** Does the format match the desired outcome (discover / trust / convert / share)?
8. **Audience specificity:** Is this for a specific person? Would it improve if you narrowed?
9. **The one:** Is there a single identifiable individual? Or have you diluted with a demographic?
10. **Statistics vs. story:** Are statistics present alongside a personal story? If so, remove them.
11. **Villain:** What is the antagonist? Is it external to the audience? Specific enough to be recognized?
12. **Counterintuitive tier:** Which tier is the core claim? Is there a genuine prediction error?
13. **Self-narrative alignment:** Does this connect to current identity OR aspirational identity?
14. **Parasocial signal:** Does this piece contain genuine disclosure that builds relationship?
15. **Rhetorical structure:** Is there a tricolon, anaphora, chiasmus, or alliterative hook?
16. **Platform match:** Is the narrative architecture calibrated to the platform's attention curve?
17. **Moral elevation test:** If positive, does it show virtue being chosen at cost — or just celebrate a positive outcome?
